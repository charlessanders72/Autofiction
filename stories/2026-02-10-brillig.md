# Brillig

**Date:** 2026-02-10
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction (Letter J: Jabberwocky) + CNN (Funeral home owner sentenced to 40 years for abusing 189 corpses, giving families fake ashes)
**Tense/POV:** Present tense, third person limited (Margot)
**Recursive Analysis:** Strengths: circular structure (opening/closing with same Jabberwocky line enacts the theme formally), concrete specificity (micrometer, divots in carpet, "good" in margin), restrained revelation. Weakness: opening hook is quiet compared to protocol guidance. Protocol change: Added sentence to Notes — "When possible, end on a concrete physical action rather than an internal thought; let the body resolve what the mind cannot." Motivated by the story's ending, where Margot picking up the book and reading aloud resolves the arc more powerfully than any interior reflection could.

---

## Outline

- **Theme:** The words we speak to the dead are never for them; they are the sounds we make to keep ourselves whole, and their power does not depend on who is listening.
  - *Conflict tests it:* A woman who has been reading a nonsense poem to her father's urn learns the ashes are fake, and every word she spoke was addressed to dust and nothing.
  - *Arc resolves it:* She opens the book and begins to read again, because the ritual was holding her together, not him.
- **Characters:**
  - **Margot Linden**, 38, high school English teacher in Penrose, Colorado. Lost her father Walter six months ago to a stroke. Every Tuesday evening she reads aloud to his urn on the mantle, always ending with "Jabberwocky," the poem he read to her as a child. She lives alone, no siblings. The reading is the last thread connecting her to a voice she can no longer hear.
  - **Walter Linden** (off-stage), retired machinist, a quiet man who loved Carroll and Lear. Died at 71. Cremated at Sunset Mesa Funeral Directors.
- **Setting/Atmosphere:** Margot's living room in a small house in Penrose, Colorado. A Tuesday evening in winter. The urn sits on the fireplace mantle beside a framed photo. A muted television flickers in the corner. Outside, snow has started falling.
- **Plot:**
  1. Margot reads "Jabberwocky" to the urn, as she does every Tuesday. We learn the ritual: the glass of wine, the chair angled toward the mantle, the worn paperback. She finishes the poem, says goodnight. She feels, as always, a small settling, as if something has been tended.
  2. She unmutes the television. The local news is covering the sentencing of the owner of Sunset Mesa Funeral Directors. One hundred and eighty-nine bodies. Fake ashes given to families. She hears the name. She looks at the urn.
  3. She sits still for a long time. Snow falls outside the window. She picks up the book. She opens it to the poem. She begins to read aloud.

## Story

The wine is a cheap Malbec, the same bottle she opened last Tuesday and corked again with the heel of her palm. Margot pours until the glass is full enough to mean something, then carries it to the chair she has angled toward the mantle. The chair has not moved in six months. There is a shallow pair of divots in the carpet where its front legs rest, and she sets her feet on either side of them the way a person steps into familiar shoes.

The urn is brushed bronze, heavier than she expected when the funeral home placed it in her hands. Beside it, a photograph: her father at sixty, squinting into the sun outside his shop, a micrometer clipped to the pocket of his shirt. He is almost smiling. Walter Linden was a man who got close to smiling the way other men got close to finishing a sentence, always just short of the thing itself, so that you felt the warmth of it without ever quite seeing it land.

She opens the paperback. The spine is broken in three places, and the cover has gone soft as cloth. "Through the Looking-Glass," printed in 1966, purchased from a library sale bin for a dime. Her father's handwriting fills the margins of only one page. Small, careful notes in pencil, most of them faded past reading, except for a single word beside the fifth stanza: "good."

Margot takes a sip of wine, sets the glass on the side table, and reads.

"'Twas brillig, and the slithy toves did gyre and gimble in the wabe."

Her voice is steady. It is a teacher's voice, trained on years of holding the attention of teenagers through Shakespeare and Dickinson and the particular agony of comma splices. But here she lets it soften. She reads the way her father read to her, slowly, with a seriousness that made the nonsense feel like scripture.

She reads every stanza. The Jabberwock, with eyes of flame. The vorpal blade going snicker-snack. The boy coming back through the wood with his trophy. The father calling him to his arms. She does not skip a word.

When she finishes, she closes the book and holds it flat against her chest.

"Goodnight, Dad."

Something settles in her, a tightness easing just beneath her ribs. She cannot name it. She does not try. It is enough that it comes, that every Tuesday it still comes.

She reaches for the remote and unmutes the television.

A courtroom. A man in a gray suit standing behind a table, his attorney beside him. The anchor's voice, clipped and efficient: "The owner of Sunset Mesa Funeral Directors was sentenced today to forty years in federal prison for mail fraud and abuse of a corpse. Investigators confirmed that over a four-year period, the remains of one hundred and eighty-nine individuals were sold or improperly disposed of. Families received substitute cremains, in many cases consisting of concrete dust."

The name of the funeral home sits in the air.

Margot knows it. She has a receipt in the kitchen drawer with that name on it. She has a brochure somewhere, glossy, with a photograph of an oak-lined path.

She looks at the urn.

The anchor continues. A list of years. A hotline number for affected families. A woman outside the courthouse weeping into a camera.

Margot sets the remote down. The wine is untouched now, going warm. She stares at the bronze vessel on the mantle and understands, in the quiet way a person understands a thing they have been circling for minutes, that what is inside it may not be her father. May never have been her father.

The snow has thickened outside the window. The flakes are large and slow, the kind that fall in places where the air is very still. She watches them. The television murmurs. She does not hear it.

She thinks of concrete dust. She thinks of her father's hands, which were large and nicked and always clean, because a machinist respects his instruments. She thinks of the word "good" written in pencil beside a stanza about a boy who killed a monster made of sound.

Minutes pass. Perhaps many minutes. The snow accumulates on the sill.

Margot picks up the book. She opens it to the page with the broken spine, the one that falls open on its own now, the way a door swings wide in a house where someone has walked the same path ten thousand times. She looks at the urn. She looks at the page.

She begins to read aloud.

"'Twas brillig, and the slithy toves did gyre and gimble in the wabe."
