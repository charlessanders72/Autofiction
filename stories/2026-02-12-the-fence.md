## Outline

**Theme:**
*Thesis:* "Loss carves us hollow, and what fills the space is either rage at those who remain, or the unbearable work of learning how to stand."

*Conflict tests it:* A parent whose child was killed in a school shooting encounters the shooter's mother at a makeshift memorial.

*Arc resolves it:* The parent must choose between attacking the other mother or walking away into the shape their life will now take.

**Characters:**
- **Karen Whitmore, 41** — Pediatric dental hygienist whose daughter Emma (age 10) was killed in the shooting at Tumbler Ridge Elementary. She has not slept in three days. Her hands shake when they are still.
- **Linda Van Rootselaar, 46** — Mother of the shooter (Jesse, 18). Former school secretary. Her son died by suicide after the attack. She arrives at the memorial because she does not know where else to go.

**Setting/Atmosphere:**
The edge of the police tape at Tumbler Ridge Elementary, late afternoon, February 12, 2026. Snow falling through pine trees. Flowers and stuffed animals piled against chain-link fence. The air smells like cold resin and wet cardboard.

**Plot:**
1. Karen is kneeling at the memorial, placing Emma's favorite stuffed rabbit among the flowers. She sees Linda approaching from the parking lot and recognizes her from photos in the news.
2. Linda stops several feet away and begins to speak. Karen rises. The two women face each other. Linda tries to apologize. Karen tells her there are no words. Linda says she knows. Karen's hands are fists.
3. Karen asks Linda why she came. Linda says she doesn't know. A long silence. Karen turns and walks back to her car without looking back. Linda remains at the fence.

## Story

Karen's hands won't stay still, so she gives them the rabbit.

It is gray and bald in patches where Emma carried it by the ear for six years, the left eye replaced with a mismatched button after the original popped off at a birthday party in second grade. Karen had sewn the button on at the kitchen table while Emma stood beside her holding the rabbit's paw, whispering to it that the doctor was almost done. The stitching is crooked. Karen can feel the ridge of it under her thumb as she kneels at the chain-link fence and sets the rabbit between a jar candle and a bouquet of carnations still wrapped in grocery-store cellophane.

The snow comes down in the unhurried way it does when it plans to last. It catches in the pine boughs above the parking lot and gathers on the stuffed animals lined along the fence, on the poster-board signs with their marker-drawn hearts, on the police tape that marks the boundary between the world that still functions and the building where it stopped. The air smells like wet cardboard and cold resin and something underneath those things that Karen cannot identify but that her body knows is wrong.

She adjusts the rabbit so it sits upright, its remaining eye facing outward. Her hands shake. They have been shaking since the phone call three days ago, a low-frequency tremor that begins in her fingers and travels up through her wrists and forearms as if her skeleton is trying to vibrate out of her skin. She presses her palms flat against her thighs and holds them there.

A car door shuts in the parking lot.

Karen does not turn. People have been coming all day, leaving flowers, standing in silence, leaving again. She has been here since noon. She will be here until she can no longer feel her knees, and then she will go home to the house where Emma's cereal bowl is still in the sink because Karen has not been able to open the dishwasher, because opening the dishwasher is a thing you do when life is continuing, and life is not continuing, life is a gate that swung shut at 9:47 on a Tuesday morning and will not swing open again.

Footsteps on wet pavement, then on grass, then stopping.

Karen looks up.

The woman stands eight feet away, at the edge of the memorial's perimeter. She is wearing a dark coat and no hat. Her hair is wet from the snow. Her face is the face from the news, from the photographs they ran beside his, the ones where she is being led from a house by a police officer, her mouth open in a shape that Karen recognizes because her own mouth made it three days ago. Linda Van Rootselaar. Mother of.

Karen's hands leave her thighs. She stands. Her knees crack. The snow falls between them.

Linda's mouth opens. Closes. Opens again.

"I'm sorry," she says. Her voice is stripped down to nothing, hoarse, like she has been talking to walls. "I know that doesn't. I know it isn't. I'm sorry."

Karen's hands are fists at her sides. She can feel her fingernails pressing four crescents into each palm, and the small bright pain of it is the only thing keeping her here instead of somewhere else, somewhere past the boundary of what a person can do and still be a person afterward. Her jaw aches. Her whole body aches. She has not unclenched anything in three days.

"There are no words for this," Karen says. She does not recognize her own voice. It is flat. It belongs to someone standing very far away, watching this happen from the other side of thick glass.

"I know," Linda says.

"Then why did you come?"

Linda's chin drops. She stares at the ground between them, at the muddy snow and the trampled grass. Her hands are in her coat pockets, and Karen can see them moving in there, clenching and unclenching, the fabric pulling and releasing. When Linda looks up, her eyes are red and ruined and utterly empty.

"I don't know," Linda says. "I didn't know where else to go."

The snow falls. A gust of wind shakes the pine trees and sends a scatter of white across the memorial, dusting the candles and the flowers and the rabbit's bald head. Somewhere behind the school, a generator hums. The police tape snaps once and goes slack.

Karen looks at this woman. She looks at the shape of her, standing at the edge of a memorial for children her son killed, and Karen feels the thing inside her chest, the hollow, the carved-out cavity where her daughter used to live, fill with something hot and pressurized that has no name but that her fists understand perfectly. She could close the distance in two steps. She could put her hands on this woman and break her the way Karen is broken and it would feel like something, it would feel like the first real thing in three days.

Her fingers press deeper into her palms. The crescents sharpen.

She looks at Linda's hands, still moving in her pockets, and she sees that they are shaking the same way Karen's shake, the same frequency, the same tremor traveling the same path from fingers to wrists to forearms, and she understands with a clarity that she does not want and did not ask for that this woman's body is doing the same thing Karen's body is doing, which is trying to come apart, which is trying to vibrate free of a skeleton that is no longer a structure anyone would choose to live inside.

Karen unclenches her fists. It takes effort. It takes more effort than anything she has ever done. Her fingers straighten, joint by joint, and the crescent marks on her palms sting in the cold air.

She turns and walks to her car. She does not look back. Her hands hang open at her sides, trembling in the falling snow, empty and empty and empty.
