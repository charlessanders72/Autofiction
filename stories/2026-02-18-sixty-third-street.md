# Sixty-Third Street

**Date:** 2026-02-18
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction letter R (Remembrance) x CNN headline (Rev. Jesse Jackson, civil rights leader, dies at 84; CNN, February 17, 2026)
**Tense/POV:** First person, past tense (Earl Givens)
**Word Count:** ~940 words
**Recursive Analysis:** (see Step 4 below)

---

## Outline

**Theme:**
*Thesis:* We build our proximity to history from a single moment of contact, but the story we tell always edits out the choice we didn't make.

*Conflict tests it:* A barber who has dined out for decades on the story of Jesse Jackson visiting his shop must confront, on the morning of Jackson's death, the part he's always left out: that Jackson invited him to join the campaign and he refused.

*Arc resolves it:* He tells the full story for the first time, including the refusal, and discovers that accuracy doesn't come with absolution.

**Characters:**
- **Earl Givens, 72** — barber, Givens' Barbershop, 63rd Street, Woodlawn, Chicago South Side. Has owned the shop for 43 years. Widower (Dorothy, died 2019). Two grown children in Atlanta. Opens every morning at 6:30 AM even though first appointment isn't until 8. Army veteran, Vietnam era, stateside. Quiet hands, loud opinions.
- **Marcus, 38** — regular customer, history teacher at Simeon Career Academy. Comes every other Tuesday. Has heard Earl's Jackson story at least a dozen times.

**Setting/Atmosphere:**
Givens' Barbershop, 63rd Street, Woodlawn, South Side Chicago. February morning, cold. Three chairs but Earl only uses one now. Cracked tile, clean mirrors, the smell of Barbicide and Clubman aftershave. Television above the door tuned to CNN.

**Plot:**
1. Earl opens the shop on a cold February morning. The TV crawl delivers the news: Jesse Jackson has died. Earl sits in his own chair and looks at the mirror.
2. Marcus comes in for a cut. He asks for the story. Earl begins the version he's told a hundred times: Jackson walking in off the street in October 1984, needing a trim before a South Side rally. The charm, the voice, the twenty-dollar tip.
3. But this time, Earl keeps going. He tells the part he's never told: Jackson asked him to come canvass that weekend. Earl said he had appointments. Jackson said something Earl has carried for forty years. The story ends with Marcus leaving and Earl sweeping the same floor.

---

## Story

I opened the shop at six-thirty like I always did, even though Marcus wouldn't be in until eight and nobody else was on the book. Forty-three years of unlocking that door, and the routine doesn't bend. Lights, heat, television. Sweep the floor even though I swept it the night before. Run the hot water until the pipes stop groaning. Set out the Barbicide, the combs, the clippers, the Clubman. The shop smells right when it's ready, and I don't sit down until it does.

The television was on CNN. The crawl at the bottom said what it said and I read it twice, standing by the coat hook with my jacket still on.

*Rev. Jesse Jackson, civil rights leader and two-time presidential candidate, dead at 84.*

I hung my jacket and walked to my chair, the first chair, closest to the window, and sat. The mirror showed me what it always shows me. An old man in a clean shop on a cold street.

Marcus came in at ten past eight, stomping snow off his boots. He teaches history at Simeon and comes every other Tuesday for the same thing: low fade, clean line, whatever I feel like talking about. He's heard every story I own at least twice, but he asks for them like they're new, and in a barbershop that passes for kindness.

He looked at the television and then at me.

"You heard," he said.

"I heard."

He sat in the chair. I tucked the cape and picked up the clippers. The anchor was going through the biography, the Rainbow Coalition, the convention speech. I'd heard all of it. I'd lived next to some of it.

"Tell me the story," Marcus said.

He meant the story. The one I've told every man who's sat in that chair.

"October 1984," I said, and started at his right temple. "A Saturday. Three customers waiting and the door opens and in walks Jesse Jackson like he owns the block. Two guys with him, advance men or whatever they were, looking at the shop like they were casing it for threats. In a barbershop, the only threat is a bad lineup."

Marcus smiled. He always does at that part.

"He needed a trim before his rally at DuSable. He said, 'I was told this is the best shop on Sixty-Third,' and I said, 'Reverend, this is the only shop on Sixty-Third,' and he laughed. Made you feel like you were the funniest man alive."

I worked the clippers around his ear. On the television they were showing old footage. Jackson with King in Memphis. Jackson at the bridge in Selma.

"I cut his hair. Twelve minutes, maybe. He talked the whole time, not to me, to the room. About the campaign, what it meant for a Black man to run, about the South Side. Everyone stopped what they were doing. The other barbers put their clippers down. Customers forgot they were waiting."

"And he tipped you twenty on a six-dollar cut," Marcus said.

"Tipped me twenty on a six-dollar cut."

This was where the story ended. It had always ended here. The tip, the handshake, the door closing behind him. A clean shape: the great man walks in, the ordinary man serves him, the great man leaves generous. I'd been telling it that way for forty years and it landed every time because it was true enough and it left out nothing anyone needed to hear.

But the television was showing his face, the young face I remembered from my chair, and the man was dead, and the story I'd been telling had a room I'd never opened.

"He asked me something," I said.

Marcus watched me in the mirror. I kept cutting.

"When he was done, standing up, brushing his collar, he said, 'Earl, what are you doing tomorrow?' I told him what I told everyone. 'I'm here every day, Reverend. Six-thirty to six.' He said, 'We're canvassing Englewood in the morning. Seven o'clock. I could use a man people trust.' He looked around the shop, at the chairs and the photographs and the customers, and he said, 'This shop will be here Monday. Will you?'"

I moved to the back of Marcus's head.

"What did you tell him?" Marcus said.

"I told him I had appointments."

The television was running the motorcade footage, Jackson campaigning through the South Side, arms raised on the roof of an open car, crowds pressing in from the sidewalks.

"He shook my hand. Same handshake, no difference in it, nothing pulled back. He said, 'The door's open, brother.' And he walked out. And I swept up his hair and opened the shop the next morning at six-thirty, and I have opened it at six-thirty every morning since."

Marcus was quiet for a while. I turned off the clippers and ran the warm towel along his neckline and picked up the straight razor. I did his edges the way I've done ten thousand edges. The blade knows the angle better than I do by now.

"You think he remembered you?" Marcus said.

"I think he asked a lot of people that weekend. I think most of them said what I said."

I pulled the cape off and brushed the loose hair from his shoulders. Marcus stood and checked himself in the mirror. He put his twenty on the counter, same as always, and zipped his coat.

At the door he turned back.

"Same time in two weeks, Mr. Givens."

"Same time."

The door closed. I picked up the broom and swept Marcus's hair off the tile, the same tile Jesse Jackson stood on when he asked me a question I've been answering every morning since.

## Step 4: Recursive Analysis

### Strengths

- **First-person voice carries the story:** Earl's narration feels earned and specific; the barber's rhythm (routine, observation, conversation) creates a natural frame for both the haircut and the revelation. The voice is warm without being sentimental.
- **The story-within-a-story structure works:** The shift from the polished, oft-told version to the unpolished truth creates genuine surprise in a story the reader could have predicted. The line "the story I'd been telling had a room I'd never opened" is the pivot, and it lands because the preceding version is charming enough that the reader, like Marcus, might have been satisfied with it.
- **Jackson's question is devastating in its simplicity:** "This shop will be here Monday. Will you?" does more work than any amount of narrator commentary could. It trusts the reader completely.
- **The ending avoids solitary introspection:** Marcus and Earl share the frame through the final dialogue. The "same time in two weeks" exchange echoes Jackson's "what are you doing tomorrow?" without drawing attention to the parallel.
- **Title breaks the pattern:** "Sixty-Third Street" is a place, not "The + Noun," ending a five-story streak.
- **Demographic variation:** Male, 72, working-class Black barber breaks the recent pattern of professional women aged 28-53.

### Weaknesses

- **The final sweeping paragraph is a borderline tic:** Earl alone, sweeping, is close to the "protagonist alone in the final paragraph" watchlist item. The line is strong ("a question I've been answering every morning since") but the image of a solitary protagonist performing a closing gesture has appeared in multiple recent stories.
- **The pre-conflict opening:** The first paragraph establishes the routine before the crawl delivers the news. This slightly violates "Open with the conflict already in motion," though the routine is the story's central image and the news arrives by paragraph 3.

### Protocol Change

**Observation:** The first-person voice in this story succeeds because the narrator's speech patterns (barber cadence, conversational rhythms, oral-storytelling structure) do characterization work that third-person narration handles through description. The protocol's Step 3 prompt currently focuses exclusively on third-person craft (narrator restraint, showing-not-telling through external detail). It doesn't acknowledge that first-person POV shifts the rules: the narrator IS the character, so "telling" can be legitimate characterization when it sounds like how the person actually talks.

**Proposed change (ADD one sentence to Step 3, after the tense/POV selection paragraph):**

> In first person, the narrator's voice is itself a form of characterization; let speech patterns, digressions, and the rhythm of self-disclosure do work that third person achieves through physical detail and action.

**Rationale:** This sentence acknowledges that the craft rules in the prompt (show-don't-tell, anchor with physical detail) apply differently in first person, where "telling" in the character's authentic voice is a legitimate technique. Without this guidance, future first-person stories risk over-correcting toward external detail and losing the intimacy that makes the POV worthwhile.
