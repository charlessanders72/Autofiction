## Threads

1. **The inadequacy of records** — Official documentation fails to hold what is specific and alive about a person; all three stories turn on this gap
2. **Preparation that does not protect** — Each protagonist rehearses or researches in advance; none of that readiness insulates them from what arrives
3. **The minimum gesture as the only possible response** — "Pull the Shin file." "Thanks for the water." Six names on a turned-over form. The week ends, each time, on the smallest adequate act.
4. **Craft: the recurring impulse to annotate** — All three recursive analyses identified and removed explanatory clauses; the project is teaching itself, again, to trust specificity

## Letter

The gallery exhaled. Judge Kim Seo-yeon did not.

That gap -- between what the room needed and what the person at the front of it could give -- is the week's central image, and it runs through all three stories without announcing itself. *Life*, *Likeness*, and *Names* occupy very different rooms: a Seoul courthouse, a Burbank conference suite, a Sierra Nevada ranger station in the hour after the radio went quiet. None of them share a plot. All of them are circling the same problem: what happens to the specific when it collides with the official, and what is lost in the translation.

The week's most insistent thread might be called the inadequacy of records. Seedance 2.0 trains on every frame of Frank Harlan's archive footage and produces something accurate enough: the MCL hitch in his knee, the jaw in the light, the careful gait on the pier. It gets one thing wrong that only Frank knows. He lit a cigarette right-handed for six years; the model gives him the lighter in his left hand. "I'll flag that with the technical team," Judith Chung says, and those eight words contain the whole argument of the story. The gap between what Frank communicated (the AI doesn't know me) and what Judith heard (there is a bug to fix) is enacted rather than explained, which is why it lands.

*Names* works the same vein from a different angle. Delphine does not know the order in which her six friends will appear in Alcazar's report. She knows the order in which she met them. Priya first, because of freshman orientation at Davis, the same lanyard color. Then Marcus, who had hated skiing the first three years and then become the best of all of them and knew it and made everyone know it, and that was fine, that was Marcus. The personal order is a counter-claim against official order: records reduce people to coordinates and weather conditions, and handing the ranger her list is the only thing she has to set against that reduction. The story's power is in knowing that this changes nothing and proceeding anyway.

What the three protagonists share, beneath their different materials, is that they were all prepared. Kim spent four months building the evidentiary record before she read it aloud. Frank had "been doing more reading lately." Delphine ran her friends' names in a loop for an hour "as if there were something to rehearse." None of this readiness is redemptive. The gallery exhales and Kim asks for the next file. Frank calls his daughter, not to describe the meeting but to hear her voice. Delphine slides the paper across the desk. Preparation turns out to be practice for endurance, not insulation. It is the capacity to keep moving after the blow, not the capacity to deflect it.

A word about what the week did not do. These three stories are controlled and quiet, and they are more similar in emotional temperature than they appear at first. The previous weeks' stories had moral complexity: characters who had known something for months and had to decide whether continued silence was still a position they could hold. This week nobody chose wrong. Kim executed her institutional role with precision. Frank declined a contract and called his daughter. Delphine gave someone names. That restraint is appropriate to all three materials, but three stories in a row ending on the smallest possible adequate gesture begin to feel like a house style rather than a choice. The minimum act is powerful when it arrives after real pressure; it risks becoming the project's reflex if the pressure is always calibrated to produce it. The work ahead may need a protagonist who swings wide and misses, or refuses the gesture entirely, to keep the register from closing.

The week introduced omniscient POV for the first time, and used it correctly: the brief passage into Alcazar's interiority, his private accounting of forty-two recoveries, his ex-wife's remark about his face, gives the reader both sides of the room before he crosses it. We see what it costs him to walk toward Delphine, and we watch her read it in real time. Only omniscient access can put the reader on both sides of a threshold simultaneously. It is a technical addition the project should use again.

Three stories, three different rooms, the same question underneath: what does the record hold, and what slips through? The week's answer, written six times on a turned-over form, was: the names, in the order that mattered. Whether that is enough the stories decline to say.
