# The Locked Thing

**Date:** 2026-02-10
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction (Letter J: Jainism) + CNN (Ukraine-Russia peace talks; Zelenskyy says Americans propose ending the war by summer)
**Tense/POV:** Present tense, third person limited (Oksana)

---

## Outline

- **Theme:** Peace built on erasure is just war with a signature, and the person who speaks both languages carries the wound in both.
- **Characters:**
  - **Oksana Mehta**, 34, Ukrainian-born translator raised by a Jain mother from Gujarat and a Ukrainian father from Kharkiv. She grew up between two traditions of non-violence: her mother's ahimsa and her father's quiet pacifism. Her father was killed in 2022 when a Russian missile struck their apartment building.
  - **Sergei Volkov**, 52, Russian deputy negotiator, career diplomat. Speaks softly, smiles often, treats every concession as though it were a gift he is offering rather than a price being extracted.
- **Setting/Atmosphere:** A windowless conference room in a hotel in Muscat, Oman. Late afternoon. Air conditioning hums. Water glasses untouched. Fluorescent light makes everyone look slightly ill.
- **Plot:**
  1. Oksana translates the opening positions. She watches Volkov's mouth form words about "territorial realities" and "security guarantees" and renders them faithfully. She notices her hands are steady. She wonders why.
  2. The Russian side presents a map. The shaded areas include her father's neighborhood in Kharkiv. Volkov calls it "a zone of shared administration." She translates the phrase. The Ukrainian delegate's pen stops. Nobody looks at her.
  3. The American mediator proposes final communique language. Both sides nod. Oksana renders the last sentence, which includes the word "reconciliation." She speaks it clearly, sets down her headset, excuses herself, and in the hallway presses her back against the marble wall and places one hand flat over her sternum, the way her mother taught her to center herself. She cannot feel her heartbeat.

## Story

The fluorescent lights in the conference room make everyone look like they are already dead, and Oksana Mehta thinks this is probably the most honest thing about the proceedings.

She sits at the narrow table between the two delegations, her headset fitted snug against her left ear, a glass of water she will not touch sweating a ring onto the mahogany veneer. The air conditioning hums at a frequency just below perception, a vibration she feels in her molars. Muscat is forty-three degrees outside. In here it could be any city, any season, any year. That is the point.

The Ukrainian delegation opens. Deputy Minister Kravchuk reads from prepared remarks, his voice flat with the particular exhaustion of a man who has said these words, or words nearly identical, in a dozen rooms like this one. Oksana renders his Ukrainian into Russian, her voice arriving half a beat behind his, a faithful shadow. She keeps her syntax clean. She does not soften. She does not sharpen. The discipline of it is something she learned not from any training program but from her mother, who used to say that a breath held with intention is not the same as a breath held in fear, even though the lungs cannot tell the difference.

Kravchuk finishes. Silence. The American mediator, Collins, makes a note on his yellow pad. Then Sergei Volkov leans forward.

He has a pleasant face. This is the first thing Oksana noticed about him when they were introduced this morning, and it unsettles her still. He looks like a man who remembers birthdays, who carries mints in his coat pocket, who would help a stranger with heavy luggage on a train. He smiles as he speaks, and the smile never reaches calculation; it simply lives on his face, native and mild.

"We must acknowledge territorial realities," he says in Russian, and Oksana opens her mouth and gives the words to the room in Ukrainian. "Security guarantees must be mutual, binding, and reflective of the situation on the ground."

Her hands are steady. She notes this with a distant curiosity, the way one might note a bird landing on a windowsill during a funeral. She expected trembling. She prepared for it, practiced breathing exercises in her hotel room this morning, her palm flat against her sternum, the way her mother taught her when she was nine and terrified of thunderstorms in Kharkiv. But her hands are still and her voice is even, and she wonders if this is composure or if something inside her has simply locked shut.

Volkov's aide unfolds a map.

It is projected onto the wall screen, but the aide also distributes paper copies, and one slides across the table to rest in front of Oksana. She looks down. The shaded regions are rendered in a pale blue, the color of a vein seen through skin. She recognizes the street grid before she reads the labels. Saltivka. The northeastern district. The apartment blocks that stood in rows like dominoes until February 2022, when they began to fall like dominoes too.

Her father's building is inside the blue.

"A zone of shared administration," Volkov says. He says it the way a surgeon might describe an incision. Necessary. Precise. Regrettable only in the abstract.

Oksana translates. "Zona spilnoho administruvannia."

Across the table, Kravchuk's pen stops moving. He does not look at her. No one looks at her. She is the glass through which light passes, and no one examines the glass.

The session continues. Collins proposes framework language. There are murmurs, recesses, sidebar conferences she is not part of. She sits and waits and drinks nothing and eats nothing, and when they reconvene she is there, voice ready, hands steady, the locked thing inside her holding firm.

By late afternoon they have reached final communique language. Both sides have agreed to a text. Collins reads it aloud in English, and Oksana renders it into Ukrainian, then into Russian, one sentence at a time, her voice carrying the words across the table like objects she is handling with gloves.

The last sentence contains the word "reconciliation."

In Russian: "primirenie." In Ukrainian: "prymyrennia." She speaks both. She makes each one clean and whole in the mouth. She gives them the exact weight the drafters intended, no more, no less.

She sets her headset on the table. The foam cushion is damp where it pressed against her ear.

"Excuse me," she says, to no one in particular, in English, which belongs to neither side.

The hallway is marble, white with grey veins, cold even through her shoes. She walks six paces from the conference room door and stops. She presses her back flat against the wall. She places her right hand over her sternum, fingers spread, the way her mother showed her. Find the heartbeat first, then slow it.

She cannot feel it.

She presses harder. The marble behind her is cool and unyielding and the hand on her chest is warm and she stands between these two temperatures, listening for the thing that tells her she is still inside her own body. The air conditioning hums. A door opens somewhere far down the corridor and closes again.

Inside the room they are shaking hands. She can hear the chairs pushing back, the murmur of voices finding their conversational registers again, becoming human. Someone laughs.

Oksana does not move. Her hand stays pressed against her chest, fingers searching, and the corridor stretches in both directions, white and endless and empty.
