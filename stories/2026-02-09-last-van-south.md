# Last Van South

**Date:** 2026-02-09
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction (Letter I: Immigration) + CNN (ICE pullback from Minneapolis after fatal shootings of two US citizens)
**Tense/POV:** Past tense, third person limited (Marcus)

---

## Outline

- **Theme:** Orders followed without question become crimes committed without conscience, until the moment someone looks you in the eye.
- **Characters:**
  - **Marcus Pruitt**, 44, a DHS Enforcement and Removal Operations agent from Virginia, deployed to Minneapolis three weeks ago. Former Army military police. Competent, disciplined, and increasingly unable to sleep.
  - **Elena Reyes**, 38, a Salvadoran-born ER nurse at Hennepin Healthcare with permanent residency. She has lived in Minneapolis for fifteen years. She treated one of the shooting victims who didn't survive.
- **Setting/Atmosphere:** A near-empty diner in South Minneapolis at 2 AM on a freezing February night, the week the federal agents were ordered to withdraw. The city is exhaling after weeks of fear.
- **Plot:**
  1. Marcus sits alone in a diner booth, unable to sleep, watching the last federal vehicles depart through the frosted window. He replays the operation that went wrong — the predawn raid, the confusion, the gunshots. He tells himself he wasn't the one who fired.
  2. Elena enters the diner after her hospital shift. She recognizes the federal jacket. A tense, quiet exchange begins. She tells him about the man who died on her table. Marcus realizes it was the same operation.
  3. Elena asks a simple question: "Do you think it was worth it?" Marcus cannot answer. She leaves. He walks out into the cold toward the last departing van.

---

## Story

The coffee had gone cold twenty minutes ago, but Marcus Pruitt kept his hands wrapped around the mug because it was the only thing keeping them still.

Through the frosted glass of the diner window, he watched the last two Expeditions idle at the curb across the street, exhaust rising white against the black of 2 AM. The rest of the convoy had already pulled out. By morning there would be nothing left but tire tracks in the slush and whatever the city decided to remember.

Three weeks. That was all it had been. It felt like something that had happened to a different person in a different country, except the country was this one, and the person was him, and the things he could not stop seeing when he closed his eyes had happened six blocks from where he sat.

The predawn raid played again without his permission. The stack at the door. The battering ram's crack, loud enough to set off a car alarm on the street. The family in the hallway, a woman screaming in Spanish, kids bare-legged on cold tile. Then the uncle, or the cousin, whoever he was, coming around the corner from the back bedroom with something in his hand that turned out to be a phone, and Royce firing twice before anyone could say a word.

Marcus had not fired. He held onto that fact the way a man treading water holds onto a piece of wood. He had not fired. He had cleared the room, he had followed protocol, he had not fired. But he had been there. He had been the one to step over the man's body to check the back bedroom, and he had found it empty except for a child's mattress on the floor and a stuffed animal with one eye missing, and he had said nothing, and they had loaded up and driven to the next address on the list.

He heard the diner door open and felt the cold press in before the hydraulic arm pulled it shut. He did not look up. He heard boots on linoleum, the rustle of a heavy coat, and then the quiet settling of someone onto a stool at the counter. The waitress, a heavy woman who had not spoken to him beyond the necessities, came alive slightly for the new customer.

Marcus glanced over. Scrubs visible beneath an unzipped parka. A woman with dark hair pulled back, circles under her eyes that matched his own. She ordered coffee, black, and pulled out her phone, then put it face down on the counter without looking at it.

She caught him watching. Her eyes went to his jacket, the one with the department patch on the shoulder, and something moved across her face. Not fear. Something harder. She looked away.

For a while they sat in their separate silences. The radiator clanked. The waitress disappeared into the kitchen. Outside, one of the Expeditions pulled away from the curb and turned south, its taillights bleeding red across the ice.

"You're still here," the woman said. She was not looking at him. She said it to her coffee.

"Leaving tonight."

She nodded. Then, as if the words had been sitting in her chest for days and simply walked out on their own, she said, "I had one of them on my table. From the raid on Bloomington Avenue. Thirty-one years old. Two kids. He coded twice and we got him back the first time, and then we didn't."

Marcus felt the wood slip from his hands.

"He kept saying a name. While we were working on him. Kept saying it over and over until he couldn't anymore." She turned on the stool and looked at Marcus directly. "Sofia. That was the name. I don't know who Sofia was. His wife, maybe. His daughter."

Marcus knew. He had heard the woman screaming it in the hallway. Not the woman's name. She had been screaming it at the man on the floor. His name. The name was not Sofia. Sofia was the girl, the one he'd found when he went back to the apartment two days later on a follow-up that wasn't really a follow-up, standing in the same hallway with a neighbor woman's arms around her, five years old, staring at him with eyes that made him understand what he had become part of.

"I'm sorry," he said. The words were nothing. He knew they were nothing.

Elena, her name tag read, turned her coffee cup in a slow circle on the counter. She did not acknowledge the apology. She was quiet for a long time, and Marcus understood that she was deciding something.

"Do you think it was worth it?" she asked.

The question sat between them like a living thing. Marcus opened his mouth. He had answers. He had been trained to have answers. National security. Rule of law. Lawful orders, duly executed. The words were all there, stacked neatly in his head the way they had been stacked in the briefings, and not one of them would come out of his throat.

Elena watched him. She did not press. She did not need to. The silence said everything that his voice wouldn't.

She finished her coffee in one long swallow, set a few dollars on the counter, and zipped her parka to the chin. At the door she paused, just for a moment, and Marcus thought she might say something else. She didn't. The cold came in, and then she was gone, and through the glass he watched her walk to her car with her head down against the wind.

He sat for another minute. Then he left a ten under his mug, pushed through the door into the shock of February air, and crossed the street to the last remaining van. The engine was running. Royce was asleep in the back seat. The driver looked at him and said, "We good?"

Marcus climbed in and pulled the door shut and said nothing, and they drove south out of Minneapolis, and the city disappeared behind them like something he would carry for the rest of his life.
