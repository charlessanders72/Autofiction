# Three Patients

**Date:** 2026-02-22
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction letter V (Vessel) x CNN headline (Trump sends hospital boat to Greenland to provide medical care for those in need; framed as humanitarian outreach while US simultaneously maneuvers for territorial acquisition; CNN, February 22, 2026)
**Tense/POV:** First person, present tense (Aviaja Olsen)
**Word Count:** ~568 words
**Recursive Analysis:** (see Step 4 below)

## Outline

**Theme:**
*Thesis:* Accepting what you need from someone who wants what you have is not charity — it is the oldest negotiation, and both sides know the terms.

*Conflict tests it:* Aviaja has three patients on existing waiting lists who need the orthopedic surgeon aboard the American hospital ship. The Danish regional liaison asks her to "facilitate the transfers" — knowing her presence will be photographed. She goes because her patients need the care.

*Arc resolves it:* She does her job. She gives a CNN journalist only clinical facts — waiting times, patient names. She walks home with Piitaq.

**Characters:**
- **Aviaja Olsen, 46** — Community health worker, Tasiilaq (Ammassalik), East Greenland. Greenlandic; Inuit mother, Danish-Greenlandic father (fisherman, deceased 2018). Twenty years navigating between Danish administrative structures and her community's actual needs. Not ideological. Her politics are her patients. Speaks Greenlandic, Danish, English. Two adult children in Nuuk.
- **Lt. Cmdr. Patricia Walsh, 38** — American military physician on the hospital ship. Orthopedic surgeon from Cleveland. First Arctic deployment. Competent, professionally warm, uncomfortable with the media handlers following her. Calls Aviaja by her practiced first name on first meeting.
- **Piitaq, 50s** — Aviaja's colleague, male nurse at the health center. Waits at the dock with a thermos of coffee. Wears a red hat his daughter knitted.

**Setting/Atmosphere:**
Tasiilaq (Ammassalik), East Greenland. February. The fjord is partly iced. The hospital ship is white against gray water, visible from the health center window. The sun goes orange by 3 PM.

**Plot:**
1. Aviaja at the health center window; phone call from the Danish liaison Lars. She names her three patients. She gets her coat.
2. Aboard the ship: the medical work. Walsh is good. Media handlers deflected twice. Aviaja translates, coordinates, advocates for her patients.
3. The dock: a CNN journalist asks for a quote about the "partnership." Aviaja gives only waiting times and names. Piitaq is waiting with coffee. They walk toward town.

## Story

The ship is white against the gray of the fjord, and I can see it from my window while I am still on the phone with Lars.

"We'd like you to facilitate the transfers," he says. "The admiral has asked for a local health liaison."

"The admiral."

"The ship's commanding officer. There's also a media team, but—"

"Lars."

"—the medical work is real, Aviaja. The surgeon they've brought, she's—"

"I know," I say. "Which patients?"

I have three. Nukaaka, sixty-one, who has had a hip problem for fourteen months and has been on the waiting list for Nuuk since last February. Old Tobias with his shoulder. And Atuarfik's youngest, the boy with the knee injury, who is not supposed to be walking on it but does anyway because he is fourteen and believes the rules of the body don't apply to him yet.

Three patients who need what is on that ship.

I get my coat.

---

They have lined the gangway with lights that will photograph warmly. A man with a camera is already at the dock. I step past him without stopping.

Lt. Commander Walsh meets me at the medical bay. She is my height, with the kind of deliberate stillness that good surgeons develop early. She shakes my hand and says, "Aviaja, thank you for coming," and I notice she already has my first name and has not asked about pronunciation, which means she practiced it. I decide not to factor this into my assessment of her.

The medical bay smells like American hospital, which is different from Danish hospital; I am not sure how. The ceiling is high enough that you don't feel the hull surrounding you.

We work for three hours.

Walsh listens to the patient before she touches them, which is not as common as it should be. She shows Nukaaka the imaging on her own screen and explains the replacement procedure in terms that let Nukaaka check the information against what she knows about her own body. When Nukaaka asks a question, Walsh stops and waits. The media handlers come through twice. The second time, Walsh holds up a hand without turning around and says, "Not now." They wait in the doorway and then go.

I translate when translating is needed. I sit with Tobias while he waits for his scan. The boy with the knee injury tries to talk me out of the surgical recommendation. I tell him Walsh has probably fixed more knees than he has stairs in his house. He looks at Walsh. He decides to trust her.

---

On the dock, a woman with a recorder steps in front of me.

"Ms. Olsen? Could you speak to what this partnership means for Greenland?"

I stop.

"Three patients received care today that they needed," I say. "All three were on existing waiting lists. Nukaaka Poulsen, fourteen months. Tobias Lynge, eight months. The boy should avoid weight-bearing for six weeks."

She nods, still recording. "And what does it mean for your community, to have this kind of American—"

"That's what I can speak to," I say. "The three patients."

I step past her.

Piitaq is at the foot of the gangway with a thermos. He is wearing his red hat, the one his daughter made him. He says nothing. He holds out the thermos.

It's coffee. We start walking toward town.

The ship is lit now, bright against the dark water. I don't look back at it.

## Step 4: Recursive Analysis

### Strengths

- **First person present tense creates immediacy:** Aviaja's decisions feel current rather than recollected. "I get my coat" is a choice happening now. The tense makes the narrator's restraint feel active rather than retrospective — she is choosing, in real time, what to observe and what to withhold.
- **Walsh's competence shown entirely through action:** She shows Nukaaka the imaging on her own screen. She waits when Nukaaka asks a question. She holds up her hand to the media handlers without turning around. The narrator never labels Walsh as skilled; the reader assembles the evidence.
- **"I decide not to factor this into my assessment of her"** — characterizes Aviaja as analytical and fair; acknowledges the political charge of a practiced pronunciation; shows her consciously setting the charge aside in order to do her actual job. Three moves in one sentence.
- **The journalist scene as counter-offer:** Aviaja won't give the journalist a feeling about partnership. She gives only what she can verify: waiting times, names, a clinical restriction. "That's what I can speak to" is both limitation and boundary. The journalist's question and Aviaja's answer are two entirely different conversations that happen in the same exchange.
- **Ending with Piitaq:** Two people in the final frame. The red hat "his daughter made him" makes Piitaq real without requiring a scene for him. The ending is forward motion — toward town, not toward the ship.
- **"I don't look back at it"** — the ship is demoted to "it," a thing Aviaja chooses not to see. The story's last argument without stating it.
- **Title "Three Patients"** — two words including a number; breaks the single-word-noun pattern of the last three titles (Names, Likeness, Life). The title is also Aviaja's only statement of principle.
- **Variation watchlist:** No fluorescent hum, no phone face-down, no protagonist alone in final paragraph, no silence as the primary climactic beat (the journalist scene ends on action and departure, not held silence). Numbers (14 months, 8 months, 6 weeks, 3 hours, 3 patients) serve as Aviaja's clinical evidence rather than decorative precision.

### Weaknesses

- **Multiple numbers in close proximity** (14 months, 8 months, 6 weeks, 3 hours, 3 patients) approach the watchlist threshold, even if each is serving Aviaja's argument. The story justifies them as clinical language — but a reader unfamiliar with the project's number-tic pattern will still encounter five measurements in a short story.
- **Three hand-related gestures** (Walsh's handshake, Walsh's hand up to the camera, Piitaq's hand holding out the thermos) — all functional, but three in 568 words approaches the watchlist item. The handshake is the least necessary; the other two earn their place.
- **Tobias is thin.** He appears in one sentence of action and is named again only in the journalist scene. Nukaaka receives a full moment with Walsh; Tobias is scaffolding. The story's structure doesn't require Tobias to be more than this, but the asymmetry is noticeable.

### Protocol Change

**Observation:** Aviaja's restraint — her choice to report events without assigning feelings to them — is itself characterization. She notes that the medical bay smells like American hospital, notes that it differs from Danish hospital, and adds: "I am not sure how." She does not tell us what she felt giving the journalist patient names instead of a sound bite. She does not tell us what she felt walking away from the ship. This withholding is the emotion; the reader fills the gap with what the surrounding context implies. The existing protocol guidance says to let first-person narrators' "speech patterns, digressions, and the rhythm of self-disclosure" do characterizing work — but addresses only what the narrator discloses. The inverse technique — restraint as disclosure, omission as emotion — is not named.

**Change made (ADD one sentence to Step 3 tense/POV guidance, after the existing first-person sentence):**

> When a first-person narrator's restraint is itself characterization, let them report events without assigning feelings to them; the omission is the emotion.

**Rationale:** The sentence addresses a specific gap: first-person narrators who withhold emotional assessment create a particular kind of reader engagement — the reader is invited to infer what the narrator will not say. This is a distinct technique from the guidance that already covers what first-person narrators do say (speech patterns, digressions, rhythm of self-disclosure). Without this note, a writer might read the existing guidance as license to enumerate a first-person narrator's feelings as long as they are embodied or observed — and miss that the choice not to enumerate can carry equal or greater weight.
