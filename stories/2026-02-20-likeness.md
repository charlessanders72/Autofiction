## Story

Judith Chung slid a sealed water bottle across the table toward him, and Frank let it sit.

"We're not talking about replacement," she said. "That's not what this is. What we're proposing is closer to restoration. Recovery of something that existed and can't be recreated otherwise."

The conference room was high up in the Meridian building, all glass on one side, Burbank spread flat and beige below. A screen at the far end. A presenter remote on the table that nobody had touched yet.

"My agent said likeness agreement," Frank said.

"That's the instrument, yes. Your performance from *Harbormaster* — the original footage, the screen tests in the archive. We'd train the model on your movement from that period. You'd retain final approval over any project where the likeness appears."

"Seedance 2.0," Frank said.

Judith's expression shifted, careful. "You've done some reading."

"I've been doing more reading lately."

She nodded. She was forty-three, maybe forty-five. When she'd apologized for the room being cold, it was one sentence, no elaboration. *Harbormaster* came out in 1996, which meant she'd been thirteen.

"Do you want to see the demo?" she asked.

He said he did.

The lights dimmed. The screen filled with a rain-slicked pier, cargo lights at the edges, a night-colored harbor behind it. A figure appeared: a man in a wool coat Frank didn't recognize, moving along the dock with the careful gait of someone who knew that surface. The shoulders were right. The slight pull in the left knee was right, a torn MCL from a pickup game in 1989 that had never fully resolved. The jaw. The way the light caught the line of it at that angle.

The figure stopped near a piling, reached into a coat pocket, produced a cigarette and a lighter. He held the lighter in his left hand.

Frank watched him light it. The gesture was smooth, unhurried, completely wrong.

He'd smoked from 1992 through 1998, Marlboro Reds until 1995, then Lights. He had quit the morning Emma was born. In those six years he had never once held a lighter in his left hand.

The lights came up. Judith was watching him.

"There's a lot to like," he said.

"The movement library is quite complete. The archive footage gave us more than we expected."

He asked about the training methodology, the exit clause, the approval window. She answered each question in order. The exit clause allowed withdrawal from any project within forty-five days of final script approval. The approval window was thirty days from delivery of each generated clip.

When she finished, he stood.

"The man on the pier," he said. "He lit the cigarette left-handed."

She waited.

"I'm right-handed. Was, back then. Every take."

Judith picked up her pen. "I'll flag that with the technical team."

"Sure," Frank said. He reached across and shook her hand. "Thanks for the water."

---

He found the Camry on level two. He sat without starting the engine, then took out his phone and called Emma.

She picked up on the third ring. "Dad. I'm at work."

"I know. Sorry."

"Is everything okay?"

"Yeah." He looked toward the far end of the structure, where the ramp curved down and the afternoon came in white and flat. "Everything's fine. I'll call you tonight."

"Okay." There was a voice in the background, someone asking her something. "Talk to you then."

"Yeah," he said. "Talk to you then."

## Step 4: Recursive Analysis

### Strengths

- **Evidence shown before action:** The demo scene establishes AI accuracy (the MCL hitch in the knee, the jaw in the light) before the error appears. The reader trusts the reproduction — which makes the lighter reveal land. When Frank names it to Judith, the reader already knows he's right; his speaking it confirms rather than reveals.
- **"I'll flag that with the technical team."** The story's best line. Corporate deflection delivered as professional courtesy. The gap between what Frank communicates (the AI doesn't know me) and what Judith hears (there's a bug to fix) enacts the thesis without stating it.
- **Phone call ends on two voices:** "Talk to you then. / Yeah. Talk to you then." Two characters in the frame at the close. Forward-moving. Frank reaching toward the living.
- **"Thanks for the water":** Small, slightly wry, dignified closing gesture in the meeting room. Earned because the water bottle was established on the first line.
- **Third person close succeeds:** Frank's interiority expressed entirely through what he notices, not through narrator labeling.
- **Demographic variation:** Male, 57, white character actor. Differs from recent protagonists in gender (Kim, Layla, Sofia), profession, and cultural context. No geopolitical or institutional backdrop.
- **Title breaks the pattern:** "Likeness" is a single noun with double meaning (legal likeness rights / resemblance to who you once were). Not "The + Noun." Recent titles: "Life," "Sixty-Third Street," "La Fortuna" — continues single-noun or place-name pattern, fine.
- **Shape variation:** Comedy-adjacent, quiet, intimate. Not a geopolitical crisis or institutional confrontation. Varies from the recent streak of high-stakes professional dilemmas.

### Weaknesses

- **Generated draft had "He sat with his hands flat on the table"** — watchlist item (pressing palms flat). Removed from final draft; the demo scene is stronger without the telegraphed signal of Frank-controlling-himself before the reveal.
- **Generated draft had "all of it already historical to her"** — annotation of an observation the context already makes. Removed from final draft. The math (she was 13 when *Harbormaster* came out) tells the reader without the addition.

The final version ends on "Yeah. Talk to you then." The parking structure detail (white afternoon at the ramp) is kept as Frank's last perception before the line ends, so the gesture remains grounded, but the story's final words belong to the dialogue.

### Protocol Change

**Observation:** The generated draft's ending followed a strong two-character phone call with "He set the phone on the seat beside him and started the car" — a brief solitary action that walked back toward isolation after the dialogue had done the emotional work. The protocol already lists "protagonist alone in the final paragraph" as a watchlist item and recommends endings with dialogue and two characters in the same frame. But it doesn't address the specific failure mode: a strong dialogue ending followed by a brief solitary coda that undoes the two-character frame. This pattern appeared here and is likely to recur.

**Change made (ADD one sentence to Notes, inserted after "Consider endings with dialogue, with two characters in the same frame..."):**

> When the emotional resolution arrives in dialogue, resist the impulse to follow it with a solitary closing action.

**Rationale:** The sentence closes a gap between the existing watchlist item ("protagonist alone in the final paragraph") and the specific failure mode: a story that *almost* ends on two voices but then retreats. Without this guidance, future stories may technically avoid "protagonist alone" as their primary beat while still defusing the two-character ending with a trailing solitary gesture.
