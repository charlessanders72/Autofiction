## Outline

**Theme:**
*Thesis:* Distance is the most profitable position in any market, until the market comes home.

*Conflict tests it:* A quantitative analyst who has built her career on abstracting political events into tradeable signals discovers that her most profitable model predicted the death of a man from her family's old neighborhood in Caracas.

*Arc resolves it:* She cannot maintain the separation between the number on her screen and the name in the news. She closes the laptop, choosing silence over signal.

**Characters:**
- **Sofia Restrepo, 28** — Quant analyst at a small prediction markets firm in Brooklyn. Born in Caracas, came to the US at six. Mathematics degree from Columbia. Models political risk. Lives alone in a walkup in Bushwick.
- **Beto Restrepo, 62** — Sofia's father. Building superintendent in Jackson Heights, Queens. Calls every Sunday. Watches Venezuelan news obsessively.

**Setting/Atmosphere:**
Sofia's apartment in Bushwick, Brooklyn. Late evening, February. Three monitors, blue light, the radiator clanking, the L train outside.

**Plot:**
1. Sofia monitors her immigration enforcement model as Minneapolis escalates. The firm's position is profitable. She logs data, keeps the news feed muted.
2. Her father calls on a weeknight. The man shot by ICE is from their old neighborhood in Caracas. Her father is crying. Sofia tries to comfort him while the trading screen updates.
3. After the call, she sits with the payout notification and the dead man's photo on adjacent screens. She closes the laptop.

## Story

The model updated at 9:47 PM, the same time it had updated every fifteen minutes since she'd set the refresh interval on Monday. Sofia watched the immigration enforcement index tick upward, 0.3 points in the last hour, 1.2 since the Minneapolis operation began. On her second monitor, the firm's position glowed green: $47,000 in unrealized gains on a $12,000 stake, leveraged against the DHS funding vote and the probability that enforcement actions would exceed Q1 targets.

She typed a note into the log: *MPL escalation tracking consensus. Model +3.1% above market. Hold.*

The third monitor showed the news feed. She kept it muted. The images were small enough to be abstract at arm's length: flashing lights, crowds, a helicopter shot of a street blocked with police vehicles. The trick was to read the data beneath the footage: deployment numbers, arrest tallies, congressional vote counts, polling shifts. The rest was atmosphere, and atmosphere didn't trade.

Her phone rang at 10:12. Not a text, a call. She checked the screen: *Papá*.

It wasn't Sunday.

"Sofi." His voice was wrong. Not the Sunday voice, the one that asked about her week and whether she was eating enough. This voice was from somewhere older. "Are you watching the news?"

"I'm watching the markets, Papá."

"The man they shot. In Minneapolis." He paused. She could hear the television in his apartment, the rapid Spanish of a Venezuelan broadcast through the phone's speaker. "His name is Andrés Fuentes. He is from La Candelaria."

La Candelaria. The courtyard of the apartment building, the sound of motorcycles, the empanada vendor on the corner whose name she'd forgotten.

"Did we know him?"

"His mother went to Iglesia San José. Your mother and his mother were in the same Bible study." He said this as though it explained everything. To him, it did. "He came here eight years ago. He had papers, Sofi. He was legal. They shot him in a parking lot."

She looked at her second monitor. The position was still green. $47,300.

"Papá, I'm sorry."

"They are saying the agents lied. That they said he had a weapon and he didn't. Your tía Marta called me. She is very upset."

"Tell tía Marta I'll find out about legal funds. I'll send you links."

"You are a good girl, Sofi."

He hung up. The apartment was quiet except for the radiator and the hum of her monitors. The L train passed outside, shaking the windows in their frames.

She looked at the trading screen. The model had refreshed while she was on the phone. The immigration enforcement index had jumped again: the investigation into the agents, the news cycle, the congressional response, all of it feeding the algorithm she'd built over six months of backtesting and calibration. The model didn't know about Andrés Fuentes. It knew about deployment vectors, media sentiment scores, legislative probability curves. It knew that escalation in one metro area predicted appropriations movement within fourteen days with 73% confidence.

She opened the position detail. The settlement trigger was the DHS funding vote, scheduled for Friday. If the vote exceeded the enforcement baseline, and her model was certain it would, because outrage and funding always moved in the same direction in an election cycle, the firm would clear $62,000. Her share was fifteen percent. $9,300 for seeing a pattern the market hadn't priced in yet.

She minimized the trading window. The news article was still open on the third monitor. Andrés Fuentes, 34. Warehouse worker. Father of two. Shot in the parking lot of a grocery store during what ICE described as a targeted enforcement action. Witnesses said he was carrying plastic bags.

She opened the photo. The polo shirt. The mustache. The way he looked directly into the camera with the expression people wear when they are told to stand still and smile, not knowing what the photo will be used for.

$47,600.

Her phone buzzed. A text from her father: a GoFundMe link for the Fuentes family. The goal was $50,000. It had raised $11,200.

She looked at the trading screen. Then at the fundraiser. Then at the photo.

Sofia closed the laptop. The screens went dark, all three at once, and the apartment settled into a quiet that had weight to it, the quiet of a room where someone has just stopped doing the only thing she knows how to do.
