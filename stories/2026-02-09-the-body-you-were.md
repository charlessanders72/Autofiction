## Story

The first thing Mika Bergstrom registers is not the pain but the quiet.

She has been under anesthesia before. Torn ACL in Cortina, hairline fracture in Garmisch. Each time she woke to noise: her coach on the phone, a physiotherapist already mapping the recovery timeline, her father's voice low and steady through the speaker telling her how many weeks, not months, how many weeks until she would be back. There was always a plan. The plan was the first thing they gave her, before water, before food, because they understood that she could not lie still without knowing when she would move again.

Now there is only the hum of fluorescent tubes and the slow beep of a monitor and, somewhere beyond the walls, a sound that takes her a moment to identify. Cheering. The evening session at the Alpine Centre. Someone is winning something.

The surgeon sits in a plastic chair beside the bed. He has not been looking at his phone or his clipboard. He has been watching her, and the fact of that, the patience of it, is what tells her before he opens his mouth.

"Mika," he says. His voice is accented, careful. "I am Dr. Petrov. I performed your surgery."

"How bad." She does not make it a question.

He leans forward slightly. He has dark eyes and the particular steadiness of someone who has done this many times and has learned that steadiness is the only kindness available to him.

"The femur fracture was compound. We repaired it with an intramedullary rod and that will heal. But the fall caused nerve damage to the peroneal nerve below the knee. Significant. We won't know the full extent for several weeks, but the imaging and what we found during the operation are consistent with permanent impairment."

Mika looks at the ceiling. It is white and featureless and tells her nothing.

"When can I ski again?"

Petrov does not hesitate, and she respects him for that. "Recreationally, with a brace and significant therapy, perhaps in eighteen months to two years. Competitively." He pauses. Not for effect. Because the word is heavy. "No. I am sorry. The nerve damage will not allow the control you need at speed. The risk of reinjury would be extreme, and no medical team would clear you."

"You don't know that," she says. The words come out flat, automatic. "You don't know what I can do in recovery. I came back from the ACL in four months. They said six."

"I know about Cortina. Your recovery was remarkable. But this is a different category of injury. The ACL is a ligament. Ligaments rebuild. Nerves, when they are damaged this severely, do not return to what they were."

She stares at him. She is looking for something in his face, some crack, some opening she can push through. She has spent her whole life finding the line no one else would take. The gap between the gates that looked impossible until she carved through it.

"I want a second opinion."

"Of course. We can arrange that."

"And I want to see the imaging myself."

"I will have it sent to your room."

She wants him to argue. She wants resistance she can push against because pushing is the thing she knows. But he only sits there with his hands folded, giving her nothing to fight, and the absence of opposition is worse than any wall she has ever hit at ninety miles an hour.

"Please leave," she says.

He stands. At the door he turns back, and for a moment she thinks he will say something about hope, or the future, or the other things doctors say when they are trying to make the wreckage easier to look at. He doesn't. He says, "The call button is on your left side. I will be here all night."

Then the door closes and she is alone.

The cheering outside swells and fades. Someone has won a medal. She can tell by the rhythm of it, the way the roar builds and crests and holds. She has been on the other side of that sound three times. She remembers the weight of the gold in her hand, the way it was lighter than she expected, the way the real weight was in her legs, in the knowledge that her legs had done something no one else's could.

She looks at the cast. It runs from mid-thigh to ankle, a white shell around the leg that has been hers since she was four years old and her father first put her on skis on the hill behind their house in Falun. She had not walked to those skis. She had run. She cannot remember a time before that running.

Her right hand works. She lifts it, opens and closes the fingers, watches them respond. This hand has held poles and trophies and her father's hand on the lift when she was small enough to be afraid of the height. She tries to think of what else it has held. A pen, sometimes. A book, rarely. She dropped out of Uppsala after one semester because the stillness of the lecture hall made her feel like she was suffocating.

What did you want to be, she thinks. Before skiing. Before Papa put you on the hill. She reaches back into the dark of early childhood, looking for some other version of herself, some girl who wanted to be a veterinarian or an astronaut or a painter, some seed of a person that existed before the speed became everything.

Nothing comes. There is no before. There is only the hill and the snow and the feeling of falling forward and choosing, every fraction of a second, not to stop.

Her phone is on the bedside table. She picks it up. Her father's name is at the top of the recent calls. He will have seen the crash. He will be waiting. She knows exactly what he will say: "Vi fixar det, Mika." We'll fix it. Because that is what they have always done. She breaks, he fixes, she goes back to the mountain.

She holds the phone and looks at his name and tries to think of what to say. Papa, it's over. Papa, I don't know who I am if I'm not this. Papa, you built me into a single thing and the thing is broken and I don't know what's left.

Her thumb hovers over the name. The fluorescent light hums. Outside, the crowd has gone quiet, the ceremony over, the athletes walking back to their rooms in the village with new metal around their necks and the whole bright continuance of their careers still stretching ahead of them.

Mika sets the phone face down on the sheet and closes her hand around nothing.
