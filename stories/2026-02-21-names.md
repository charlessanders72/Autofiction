## Outline

**Theme:**
*Thesis:* Survival is not a reward for staying; it is only the next thing that happens to you.

*Conflict tests it:* Delphine stayed behind at the lodge with a twisted ankle while her six friends went for an afternoon backcountry run. She called search and rescue when they didn't return by dark. She's been at the ranger station for six hours. Ranger Alcazar is crossing the room toward her now.

*Arc resolves it:* She receives the news. She asks one question. She returns to the main room and writes six names in the order she met them, not in any official order, just the true one. She gives the paper to the ranger. The names enter the record.

**Characters:**
- **Delphine Osei-Bonsu, 31** — PE teacher at a Sacramento middle school. Ghanaian-American. The group has come skiing in the Sierra Nevada every February since 2017; this was the ninth year. She twisted her ankle during the morning session and watched the others go up from the lodge window. She is not a worrier by nature. She became one at 4:47 PM when they didn't come off the mountain.
- **Ranger Keith Alcazar, 44** — Search and rescue coordinator, Inyo National Forest. Twenty-three years in this work. Forty-two recoveries. He has gotten better at the forms and the phone calls. Not at the walking across a room.

**Setting/Atmosphere:**
Inyo National Forest ranger station, Mammoth Lakes, California. February evening, past sunset. The station smells of wet wool and coffee that has been on too long. Outside the window, snow has stopped; a quarter moon lights the treeline.

**Plot:**
1. Delphine in the waiting area: the radio went quiet two hours ago. Alcazar comes through the hall door. She stands.
2. The exchange: he delivers the news. She asks one question. He answers.
3. She returns to the main room, asks for paper, and writes six names in the order she met them. She hands the paper to the ranger.

## Story

The radio had been quiet for two hours.

Delphine had learned the station's rhythms without trying: check-ins every forty minutes, the voice on the mountain, flat responses from the woman at the dispatch desk. Sometime after six the voice on the radio said something to the dispatcher, and the dispatcher pulled off her headset, and after that the radio was quiet. The station smelled of wet wool and coffee that had been on too long. Outside the window the snow had stopped. The mountains were lit by a quarter moon, white and still above the treeline, and she had been looking at them long enough that the shapes had changed: not beautiful, just large.

The door at the end of the hall opened.

She stood.

---

Keith Alcazar had done this forty-two times and had gotten no better at the walking. He had gotten better at other things: the phone calls to next of kin, the briefings, the reports with their precise language that turned people into coordinates and weather conditions. But crossing a room toward someone who was still waiting — that part had not improved. His ex-wife had told him once that his face was an open book, and he had thought she was wrong. He was less sure now.

She was standing before he reached the door. She still had her ski boots on; she hadn't been able to get them off with the ankle.

She was reading his face. He could tell.

"Ms. Osei-Bonsu," he said.

---

She had been saying their names in order for the last hour. Priya first, because she had known Priya the longest: freshman orientation at Davis, same group, same lanyard color. Then Marcus, Priya's husband, who had hated skiing the first three years and then become the best of all of them and knew it and made everyone know it, and that was fine, that was Marcus. Then Joy, Tunde, Dev, Angela. In order of meeting. She had run the loop six times, as if there were something to rehearse.

"All of them?" she asked.

"Yes." He stopped. "I'm sorry."

"I know," she said.

---

She went back into the main room. The young ranger who had taken her initial report was still at his desk. She walked to him.

"I need a piece of paper."

He glanced past her to Alcazar, who was still in the doorway. Then he opened a drawer and pulled out a blank form, turned it over.

She sat down. He put a pen on top of the paper.

She wrote Priya first. Then Marcus. Then Joy, Tunde, Dev, Angela.

She slid the paper across the desk.

"Those are the names," she said.

He took the paper. Alcazar, at the door, didn't move.

## Step 4: Recursive Analysis

### Strengths

- **Omniscient POV used to genuine effect:** The brief shift into Alcazar's interiority (his ex-wife's observation, his private accounting of forty-two recoveries) gives the reader two perspectives on the same crossing without disrupting the story's unity. We see him from the inside as she reads him from the outside. The omniscient access is earned by the story's dramatic structure: the reader needs to understand what he is carrying across that floor before he arrives.
- **"All of them?"** — Three words that demonstrate Delphine has already done the work. The question is for confirmation, not information. The preceding scene (her running names in a loop "as if there were something to rehearse") earns the question.
- **Marcus in two clauses:** "who had hated skiing the first three years and then become the best of all of them and knew it and made everyone know it, and that was fine, that was Marcus" gives a dead man more specificity than some stories give their protagonists. The affection is in the accumulation, not in a stated emotion.
- **Naming in meeting-order:** Priya, Marcus, Joy, Tunde, Dev, Angela. Personal order against official order. The contrast is implied by the preceding scene (she explicitly notes "in order of meeting") and enacted by the writing. The reader connects them.
- **Demographic variation:** Delphine, 31, Ghanaian-American, PE teacher. Youngest protagonist in recent memory; no institutional/professional dilemma; grief without a villain; natural disaster. Differs from recent protagonists in age bracket, profession, cultural background, and story shape.
- **Title:** "Names" is a plural noun, no article, resonant double meaning (the act of writing them; the story of what names are for). Not "The + Noun." Breaks from recent single-noun titles ("Life," "Likeness") by being plural and more grammatically open.
- **Variation watchlist:** No hands as dominant body language, no fluorescent hum, no phone face-down, protagonist not alone in the final paragraph (young ranger and Alcazar both present), dialogue carries the climactic beat. Omniscient POV is new to the project.

### Weaknesses

- **Early draft included "In the order she had met them, which was not alphabetical and not the order they would appear in any report"** after writing the six names. This clause annotated what the list already communicated: the meeting-order had been established in the interior monologue scene; the reader knows the official order would differ. The clause was cut before final draft. The names list stands without commentary.
- **"He was less sure now."** This sentence closes Alcazar's reflection on his ex-wife's remark. It works, but it is the one moment the narrator is close to explaining a character's private doubt rather than showing it. On balance it earns its place because the story's argument depends on understanding what his walk across the room costs him, and the surrounding context keeps it lean.
- **Alcazar is rendered more abstractly than Delphine.** His physical presence in the room is thin. He has interiority and function, but no specific body beyond "didn't move." A second pass might give him one more physical anchor — the specific floor under his boots, the weight of the radio transcript in his pocket — to match the texture of Delphine's waiting.

### Protocol Change

**Observation:** The early draft followed the act of writing six names in meeting-order with this sentence: "In the order she had met them, which was not alphabetical and not the order they would appear in any report." The clause explains a contrast that the preceding interior monologue had already established and that the list itself enacts. The existing protocol notes address this impulse for metaphors ("When a metaphor carries its meaning on its own, do not add a preceding or trailing clause that explains it") and for flashback framing, but not for the analogous case: a climactic action whose meaning lives in a specific detail — the particular order, the exact word, the chosen object. The same impulse to explain appears in all three cases, but a writer can observe the metaphor rule and still annotate a final action.

**Change made (ADD one sentence to Notes, after the metaphor annotation sentence):**

> When a climactic action derives its meaning from a specific detail — the exact word, the particular order, the chosen object — cut any following clause that explains what that detail means; the specificity is the argument.

**Rationale:** This sentence closes a gap between the existing metaphor rule and the methodology rule. It names the same impulse applied to a third category: a story's final action (or any action whose meaning lives in particularity). Without this note, a writer can satisfy both existing rules and still add a sentence explaining why a character wrote names in that order, chose that object, or said that word. The sentence makes the principle portable beyond metaphors and expertise.
