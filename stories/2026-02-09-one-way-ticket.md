# One Way Ticket

**Date:** 2026-02-09
**Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction (Letter I: Incarceration) + CNN (China critic Jimmy Lai sentenced to 20 years in prison)
**Tense/POV:** Past tense, third person limited (Mei)

---

## Outline

- **Theme:** Distance is a luxury purchased with silence, until the moment you realize you've been rationing your own heart, and the price of one more goodbye becomes the price of never having said it at all.
  - **Conflict tests it:** Mei receives the sentencing news and must choose between safety in London and one final visit to her imprisoned father in Hong Kong.
  - **Arc resolves it:** She books the ticket, knowing she may not return, because the alternative—never seeing him again—is a sentence she refuses to serve.
- **Characters:**
  - **Mei Lai**, 34, freelance translator living in London who left Hong Kong in 2020 after the National Security Law passed, carries her father's words in everything she translates but has not heard his voice in three years.
  - **Jimmy Lai** (off-stage presence), her father, the imprisoned journalist whose conviction she learns about through a WhatsApp message from her aunt.
- **Setting/Atmosphere:** Mei's small flat in Brixton, London, late Sunday night; rain against the windows, the blue glow of her laptop screen, a half-packed suitcase on the bed.
- **Plot:**
  1. Mei receives the sentencing news via WhatsApp. Twenty years. She sits at her desk, paralyzed, staring at flight prices to Hong Kong on her laptop.
  2. Internal conflict unfolds through small actions: she pulls out her suitcase, then pushes it back under the bed. She calls her aunt, who warns her not to come. She re-reads her father's last letter from 2023.
  3. She books a one-way ticket. She does not pack. She sits with the confirmation email on her screen, knowing what it means, knowing she would rather carry this choice than the alternative silence.

---

## Story

The WhatsApp message arrived at 11:47 PM, three words from her aunt in Hong Kong: *Twenty years. Final.* Mei Lai read it twice, then set her phone face down on the desk beside her laptop, where a Cathay Pacific booking page glowed in the dark. The cursor blinked in the "Departure Date" field. Rain ticked against the window of her Brixton flat, steady and indifferent.

She had opened the booking page two hours earlier, when the sentencing hearing was supposed to end. She had not touched the keyboard since.

Her father was sixty-seven. Twenty years meant he would be eighty-seven if he lived that long. The arithmetic was simple and final, the kind of math that left no room for optimism or bargaining. Mei closed her eyes and saw him the way she always did: not as he must look now, thinner and grayer after four years in custody, but as he had looked the last time she saw him in person, summer of 2020, standing at the baggage claim in Heathrow wearing a blazer too warm for the weather and carrying a hardback copy of *A Tale of Two Cities* he'd bought at the airport because he always bought books at airports.

She stood and walked to her bedroom. The suitcase lived under the bed, a black roller bag she had used once since moving to London, a trip to Edinburgh for a conference she had not wanted to attend. She pulled it out. The wheels squeaked. She set it on the bed and unzipped it and then stopped, hands resting on the empty fabric interior, and did not move for a long time.

Her phone rang. Not WhatsApp. A real call. The number had a Hong Kong country code. She answered.

"Mei."

Her aunt's voice was careful, flattened by exhaustion or caution or both. In Hong Kong, phone calls were never just phone calls anymore.

"I got your message," Mei said.

"Good. Listen to me. Do not come here."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Liar. I can hear it in your voice."

Mei sat on the edge of the bed beside the suitcase. The rain had picked up. She could hear it through the phone too, faint static in the background of her aunt's breathing.

"They will not let you see him," her aunt said. "Not for months, maybe longer. And if you come, if they see your name on a passenger manifest, you know what happens next."

"I know."

"Then why are we having this conversation?"

Mei looked at the suitcase. She had not put anything in it. She had not even decided what she would bring. Passport, phone, a week's worth of clothes. That was all anyone needed to disappear.

"I don't know," Mei said.

Her aunt was silent. Then: "He told me to tell you something. Before the sentencing. He said if it went badly, I should tell you he doesn't regret any of it. Not the writing, not the newspaper, not the trial. He said you should live your life and not look back."

"He said that."

"Yes."

"In those words."

"Mei."

"He wouldn't say it that way. He would say something about Orwell or Havel or some writer I've never heard of and expect me to figure it out."

Her aunt laughed, a short, brittle sound. "He said you would say that. He said to tell you it's from Camus. 'The only way to deal with an unfree world is to become so absolutely free that your very existence is an act of rebellion.' He said you'd know what he meant."

Mei's throat tightened. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and said nothing.

"Don't come," her aunt said again, and hung up.

Mei sat in the dark for a long time. Then she stood, walked back to her desk, and opened the desk drawer. Inside was a letter on thin blue airmail paper, creased from being folded and unfolded too many times. She did not need to read it. She had memorized it three years ago. But she unfolded it anyway and held it under the desk lamp.

*Mei, if you're reading this, I am likely somewhere I cannot write to you from. Do not be angry. I knew the odds when I stayed. You knew them when you left. We both made the only choices we could live with. That is not tragedy. That is clarity.*

*Live well. Speak truly. Do not spend your life translating other people's words while swallowing your own.*

*All my love, Ba.*

She folded the letter and set it on the desk. She returned to the laptop. The booking page was still open, the cursor still blinking. She clicked on the "Departure Date" field and selected the earliest available flight: Tuesday morning, 10:35 AM.

One way. £847.

She entered her passport number. She entered her credit card. She did not pack the suitcase. She did not call anyone. She clicked "Confirm Booking," and the page refreshed, and the confirmation email arrived eighteen seconds later with a PDF boarding pass attached.

Mei opened the attachment. She looked at her name printed in Helvetica beside the departure gate and seat number, 47A, a window seat, and she thought about her father sitting in a cell and her aunt's warning and the way the rain sounded against the window, the same rain that was falling in both cities, London and Hong Kong, separated by six thousand miles and a border she might not cross twice.

She closed the laptop. She did not move the suitcase off the bed. She sat at her desk in the dark and waited for morning.
