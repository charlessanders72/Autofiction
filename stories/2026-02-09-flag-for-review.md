# Flag for Review

## Metadata

- **Date:** 2026-02-09
- **Prompt Source:** Daily Micro Fiction letter I (Infohazards) + CNN headlines (famous names in Epstein files; GOP lawmaker says Commerce Secretary Lutnick should resign over Epstein links)
- **Tense/POV:** Present tense, third person limited (Nadia)

## Outline

### Theme/Thesis

The truth doesn't care who it hurts, and knowing it makes you complicit in either its burial or its consequences.

- *Conflict tests it:* A young researcher finds her mentor's name in damning documents and must decide whether professional loyalty or moral clarity wins.
- *Arc resolves it:* She realizes that protecting him means becoming what he is — someone who knew and said nothing.

### Characters

- **Nadia Vasquez** (31) — Junior legal researcher at a nonprofit document review project, two years out of law school, meticulous and rule-following. Owes her career to Judge Emory Sands, who wrote her law school recommendation and hired her for her first clerkship.
- **Judge Emory Sands** (67, referenced but not present) — Federal appellate judge, widely respected, Nadia's mentor since she was an undergrad. His name appears on a flight manifest.

### Setting/Atmosphere

A fluorescent-lit document review room in downtown D.C., late on a Tuesday night. Stacks of binders, cold coffee, the hum of overhead lights. Nadia is alone; the rest of the team left hours ago.

### Plot

1. Nadia is deep in a routine batch of flight manifests when she sees a name she knows. She reads it three times. Her hands stop moving. She looks around the empty room.
2. She pulls up his public schedule from that year, cross-references the dates. They match. She thinks about calling him, then realizes what that call would mean. She thinks about her clerkship, his recommendation letter, every door he opened. She considers whether the log could be innocent.
3. She stares at the "flag for review" button on her screen. She thinks about what happens if she doesn't click it: she goes home, sleeps, comes back tomorrow, and the name is just another line in a spreadsheet someone else will find. Or won't. She clicks it.

## Story

The name is small. Ten-point font, maybe eleven, wedged between a Brazilian commodities broker and a retired state senator's wife. Nadia's index finger hovers above it on the screen, as if touching the glass might make it more real or less. She reads it once, then again, then a third time, her lips barely forming the syllables she has known since she was nineteen years old.

Emory R. Sands.

The fluorescent lights above her flicker in their usual half-second stutter, the one the building manager has promised to fix since October. The document review room is empty. Has been since eight, when Rafael packed up his binders and told her she was going to ruin her eyes. That was three hours ago. The coffee in her mug has gone the color of dishwater, and a faint ring marks where it sat too long on a photocopied deposition page.

She scrolls back to the header of the manifest. The flight originated from Teterboro, a private airstrip in New Jersey, on March 14, 2017. Destination: a small airfield outside Nassau. The document is part of a batch flagged during a federal investigation into a financier whose name she first encountered six weeks ago, along with everyone else on the team. The manifests are routine. Most of the names mean nothing. They are supposed to mean nothing.

Nadia pushes back from the desk. The wheeled chair rolls unevenly across the carpet tile, and she catches herself with her heel. She looks at the door, then at the window, where the lights of K Street bleed pale and orange through the blinds. There is no one to catch looking. There is no one here at all.

She pulls her laptop closer and opens a second browser tab. The federal judiciary's public calendar archives load slowly on the nonprofit's aging Wi-Fi. She types his name. She already knows his schedule from that year better than she should, because she clerked for him the year after, and he liked to reference old cases the way other people referenced weather. But she needs to see it laid out, needs the dates to either match or not match, because if they don't, this is a clerical error, a common name, a ghost.

March 14, 2017. No oral arguments scheduled. No conference notes. A gap in the calendar, clean and white, the kind of empty square that could mean anything. She checks the days before and after. He sat on a panel on the twelfth. Another on the sixteenth. The fourteenth is a breath held between two ordinary weeks.

She thinks about calling him. She has his personal number, the one he gave her when she graduated, the one she used exactly once, to thank him for the letter that got her into Georgetown. She imagines the phone ringing in his study, the walls lined with maritime prints, the brass lamp he never replaced even after his wife told him it was hideous. She imagines him answering, his voice low and unhurried, the way it always is, and she imagines saying, Judge, I found your name, and she imagines the silence that would follow.

That silence would be a door. On the other side of it, she is someone who warned him. Someone who gave him time.

Her hand stays on the desk.

She thinks about the recommendation letter. She has never read it, but she knows what it must have said, because every interviewer who held it looked at her differently. She thinks about the clerkship, the way he let her draft a concurrence that other clerks spent their entire terms hoping to touch. She thinks about the door to this career, and who opened it, and whether the person who opened it can also be the person on that manifest, and whether those two things can exist in the same man without one canceling the other.

She knows they can. That is not the question.

The question is on her screen, in the shape of a small gray button that reads FLAG FOR REVIEW. It sits in the toolbar beside SKIP and MARK IRRELEVANT, and she has clicked it four hundred times in the last six weeks for names that meant nothing to her. Senators' staffers. Corporate officers. People who existed only as strings of text between a departure point and an arrival, whose lives she would never intersect with, whose fates she would never learn.

If she does not click it, she goes home. She takes the 11:40 Metro, sits in the half-empty car, watches her own reflection in the dark window. She comes back tomorrow, opens a new batch, and this manifest slides into the archive with ten thousand others. Maybe someone else finds it. Maybe no one does. Maybe the name sits there for years, small and patient, waiting in ten-point font.

And she will know it is there. She will know it every time she sees his face on C-SPAN, every time a former clerk sends her an alumni newsletter, every time someone tells her how lucky she was to work for him. She will carry the knowledge like a stone in her shoe, not heavy enough to stop her from walking, just heavy enough that she never forgets it is there.

She thinks about the people on that island. She does not know their names. She does not know their faces. But she knows why the manifests matter, because she sat through the briefing like everyone else, and she saw the photographs pinned to the corkboard in the conference room, and she understood what those flights were for.

Nadia puts her hand on the mouse. The cursor drifts to the button. She can feel her heartbeat in her fingertips, a dull, insistent rhythm against the plastic.

She clicks it.
