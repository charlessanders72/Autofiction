## Story

Layla Shirazi plugs in her headset at 8:47 and the room across the wall goes live.

She can hear the Americans settling, the scrape of chairs, someone asking for more water. Her booth is a glass rectangle suspended between two delegations that have not been in the same room in forty-three years and are not in the same room now. That is the point of her. She is the room between the rooms.

The Farsi channel crackles open and Hossein Farrokhzad begins his morning remarks without preamble, without the diplomatic softening she has learned to expect from men his age. His Tehran formal Persian is precise, the verbs conjugated as if conjugating them incorrectly would be its own kind of concession. She thinks, as she always does in the first thirty seconds, of her father reading the Shahnameh aloud on Sunday mornings in their Bethesda kitchen. The same care. The same refusal to slur.

She translates into her microphone in the same breath he speaks. She does not pause to admire his Farsi. She does not editorialize. Her father's rule, which she heard first at age nine when he caught her softening a message to her grandmother: *Say what the man said, not what you wish he said.*

The session runs two hours without incident. Iran's position on enrichment limits. America's position on the timeline for sanctions relief. Farrokhzad reads from a paper with the flat affect of a man who knows his paper will be picked apart later and sees no reason to help. Layla translates. The American delegation types. Outside the tall windows the lake is a color between pewter and nothing.

At 11:12 Farrokhzad looks up from his paper.

She knows this because his voice changes. The read-aloud formality drops and something else enters, the unscripted register, and she sits forward without meaning to. She can see his delegation across the atrium glass but not his face, only the posture of deference that the others in the room adjust to when their senior man speaks without a script.

He says, in Persian, that the Supreme Leader's position is not a negotiating floor.

The literal translation is: *This is the ceiling, not the floor.*

The diplomatic translation, the one that would travel most safely from her microphone to the American earpieces, would be something like: *These limits are fixed.* Firm, but structural. A wall, not a challenge.

The literal one is a ceiling. A ceiling implies there is a room below it. A room below it implies there is space being defined and defended, which is different from a wall, which just stops you. A ceiling says: *you thought you were negotiating up, but there is no up.* It is not a threat. It is a correction. It is also, in context, the kind of correction that makes a delegation go very still.

She has translated worse. She has rendered three words of Farsi that ended a sub-session in 2021. She is not afraid of consequences.

She is afraid of her father's voice in her ear.

*Say what the man said.*

"The Supreme Leader's position," she says into her microphone, in the same flat affect she has used for two hours, "is not the floor of negotiations. It is the ceiling."

The American side goes quiet. Not the quiet of note-taking. The quiet of a room realizing it needs to confer without her.

At 11:19 the American delegation head says, into his own microphone, "We'll call a short recess."

Layla takes the headset off and places it on the desk. Through the glass the Americans are already pulling phones from jacket pockets, turning away from each other for privacy in a room that has none. She cannot see Farrokhzad's room from here. She wonders if he knows what his ceiling has done to the next hour, the next week, whether this recess becomes a rupture. She wonders if he said it because he knew.

She opens her thermos and pours cold coffee into the plastic cap and drinks it.

The room between the rooms is very quiet.

Her father called her last night from Bethesda. She let it go to voicemail. He would have asked her how it was going and she would have said fine. He would have said, *just remember, you are not the diplomat, you are the voice.* He said that every time she worked a big session, the same sentence, because he believed it and because he needed her to believe it and because neither of them had ever agreed on whether it was a comfort or a warning.

She picks up the headset. No one is speaking yet on either channel. She puts it on anyway.

Outside, the lake is still the same color.
