# Story Trends Catalog

**Date:** 2026-02-18
**Scope:** All 17 stories generated from 2026-02-09 through 2026-02-17

---

## Unusually Recurring Trends

### 1. Female protagonists (15 of 17 stories)

Only two stories have male protagonists: Dariush in "The Clean Text" and Marcus in "Last Van South." Every other story centers a woman. This is the most statistically skewed pattern in the corpus.

### 2. "The ___" title formula (13 of 17 stories)

Thirteen stories use a definite-article title (The Clean Text, The Locked Thing, The Shape It Takes, The Payout, The Fence, The Body You Were, The Measure, The Draft, The Shot, The Green Room, The Architecture, The Coleslaw, The Interpreter). Only "Brillig," "Flag for Review," "One Way Ticket," and "Last Van South" break the pattern.

### 3. Third person limited POV — no exceptions

All 17 stories use close third person limited. No first person, no omniscient, no second person, no multi-POV. The tense splits roughly 10 present / 7 past, but the POV never varies.

### 4. The Epstein scandal used three times

Three separate stories use the Epstein case as their moral fulcrum:
- **Flag for Review** (2026-02-09): Nadia finds her mentor Judge Sands on a flight manifest.
- **The Architecture** (2026-02-13): Claire remembers a girl at a 2004 dinner party; resigns.
- **The Draft** (2026-02-16): Rachel drafts a resignation over Goldman Sachs/Epstein ties.

The recursive analysis on "The Draft" explicitly flagged this: "the recurring use of Epstein as the moral fault line risks making it feel like the protocol's default setting for institutional complicity stories."

### 5. US-Iran diplomacy / negotiation settings (3 stories)

- **The Clean Text** (2026-02-09): Iran nuclear deal, Muscat, Oman.
- **The Locked Thing** (2026-02-10): Ukraine peace talks, Muscat, Oman.
- **The Interpreter** (2026-02-17): US-Iran nuclear talks, Geneva.

Muscat, Oman is a recurring specific setting (2 stories).

### 6. ICE / immigration enforcement in Minneapolis (2 stories)

- **Last Van South** (2026-02-09): ICE agent reflects on a fatal raid.
- **The Payout** (2026-02-16): Quant analyst profits from the same Minneapolis operations.

Both reference the same underlying event from different angles.

### 7. The hands motif — near-universal

Almost every story devotes disproportionate attention to what characters do with their hands:
- **The Fence**: shaking hands, fists, crescent marks in palms; Linda's hands shaking identically.
- **The Body You Were**: "closes her hand around nothing."
- **The Locked Thing**: steady hands, hand pressed to sternum.
- **Flag for Review**: heartbeat in fingertips against the mouse.
- **The Architecture**: hands flat on desk "the way fighter pilots steady themselves against g-force."
- **Last Van South**: hands around mug "because it was the only thing keeping them still."
- **The Measure**: surgeon's steady hands, father's hands.
- **The Draft**: "Her fingers are steady."
- **The Clean Text**: hands around documents.
- **Brillig**: book held flat against chest.

### 8. Phone placed face-down (4+ stories)

A repeated specific gesture:
- **The Clean Text**: "his phone face-down"
- **The Body You Were**: "Mika sets the phone face down on the sheet"
- **One Way Ticket**: "set her phone face down on the desk"
- **Last Van South**: "put it face down on the counter"

### 9. Fluorescent lights humming (5 stories)

Used as a sensory/atmospheric marker:
- **The Body You Were**: "fluorescent tubes," "fluorescent light hums"
- **The Locked Thing**: "fluorescent lights...make everyone look like they are already dead"
- **Flag for Review**: "fluorescent lights above her flicker"
- **The Shot**: "fluorescent light buzzed at a frequency Dr. Rao had stopped noticing"
- **The Green Room**: "fluorescent lights hum at a frequency Meredith can feel in her molars"

### 10. Protagonist alone at the end — nearly universal

Almost every story ends with the main character in isolation: sitting alone, walking alone, standing alone in a corridor, driving in silence. The final image is solitary in at least 14 of 17 stories.

### 11. Moral/ethical dilemma through professional role — the universal structure

Every story frames its conflict through the protagonist's professional identity: diplomat, translator, doctor, nurse, quant analyst, legal researcher, corporate attorney, news anchor, law enforcement agent, pediatrician, skier. The tension is always institutional duty vs. personal conscience.

### 12. Dead, imprisoned, or absent family members

Protagonists disproportionately carry the weight of lost or absent family:
- Dariush: brother killed (Iran-Iraq war)
- Margot: father dead
- Iryna: husband and three children killed
- Karen: daughter killed
- Dr. Lena Reyes: mother died last year
- Mei: father imprisoned
- Maya: daughter kept away for protection
- Marcus: haunted by a dead man's child

### 13. Hyper-specific numbers and measurements

Stories compulsively use precise rather than round figures: "forty-three times," "ten-point font," "9:47 PM," "$47,300," "four hundred meters," "ninety seconds," "eleven seconds long," "fourteen laughing emojis," "sixty-two days," "8:47," "11:12"

### 14. Silence as climax

Many stories reach their peak emotional moment through deliberate silence or refusal to speak:
- Catherine says nothing and leaves (The Clean Text)
- No one drinks (The Coleslaw)
- Oksana cannot feel her heartbeat (The Locked Thing)
- Marcus cannot answer (Last Van South)
- Meredith walks out without speaking (The Green Room)
- Nadia clicks without speaking (Flag for Review)

---

## Summary

The strongest and most notable patterns are:
1. The near-total dominance of female protagonists (88%)
2. The identical POV choice across all 17 stories
3. The Epstein scandal appearing three times as a moral engine
4. The "hands" motif functioning as an unconscious authorial signature
5. The universal structure of professional-role moral dilemma resolved in isolation
